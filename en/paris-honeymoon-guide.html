Romantic things to do in Paris

Catch the Eiffel Tower's hourly sparkle from somewhere with room to breathe

After dark, the Tower sparkles for five minutes at the top of every hour, roughly until 11pm. Standing directly underneath is dramatic but genuinely crowded — the esplanade at Trocadéro, across the river, puts the whole tower in frame with grass to sit on and far fewer people jostling for the same angle. No ticket needed; it's a public square, open around the clock.

Take a sunset cruise, but time it properly

Sunset river cruises typically depart around an hour before sundown, so you get gold light on the way out and the city lighting up for the return leg — a plain "evening" cruise later on won't give you that transition. Boats leave from a handful of points along the Seine, most commonly near the Eiffel Tower, Pont de l'Alma, and Pont Neuf. Evening departures are the first to sell out from late spring through summer, so book at least a week ahead if the date matters to you.

Go up for free instead of paying for an observation deck

The rooftop terrace at Printemps, the department store on Boulevard Haussmann, is open to anyone during store hours with no ticket and no line — and it looks out over the rooftops to the Eiffel Tower, Opéra Garnier, and Sacré-Cœur all at once. Part of the terrace turns into a paid seasonal bar in the warmer months, but the free viewing area stays open regardless.

Walk the bridge that used to be a mountain of padlocks

Pont des Arts isn't where you'd leave a lock anymore — the city removed close to a million of them in 2015 after the accumulated weight helped collapse a section of railing, and replaced the grilles with glass panels to stop it happening again. What's left is one of the best straight-on views of the Louvre and the Institut de France, on a pedestrian bridge that's considerably calmer than it was in its lock-covered years.

See Notre-Dame finished, not scaffolded

The cathedral reopened in December 2024 after its post-fire restoration — spire included — and the exterior is fully visible again from the square out front or the quays on the Left Bank. Worth a slow pass even if you don't go inside; for years, every photo of it included construction cranes, and that's no longer the case.

Proposal locations in Paris

All of these are public spaces — no venue booking, no fee, no permission needed. What varies is how crowded they get and when.

Square du Vert-Galant

A small triangular park at the western tip of Île de la Cité, right at the waterline where the Seine splits around the island. It's open around the clock and free, and in practice it's mostly joggers and the occasional bottle of wine on a bench — genuinely quiet for somewhere this central. The view runs straight down the river toward Pont Neuf.

The gardens at Palais Royal

A formal garden enclosed by arcades just north of the Louvre, with the striped Buren columns in the front courtyard. Entry is free and the gates are open from early morning into the evening, with exact hours shifting by season. Because most visitors don't realize it's there, it stays noticeably calmer than the Louvre courtyard five minutes away.

Pont Alexandre III

The single most ornate bridge in Paris — built for the 1900 World's Fair and named for the Russian tsar, with gilded winged horses on each pier and a clear line of sight to the dome of the Grand Palais. It's a public bridge with no restricted hours; the only real downside is that you'll usually be sharing the view with other people, especially around golden hour.

Trocadéro — but early or late, not midday

The esplanade facing the Eiffel Tower is free and technically open 24 hours, but between roughly 10am and early evening it's dense with tour groups and souvenir sellers. Show up near sunrise, or after most groups have cleared out for dinner, and it becomes a genuinely different, much more private place to ask.

Best sunrise locations for couples

Same logic behind all three: these are places that are actively unpleasant with crowds by mid-morning, and completely different at dawn.

Trocadéro

The esplanade faces roughly southeast toward the tower, so early sun comes in from the side rather than straight into your eyes or your camera — you get warm light on the stone and the ironwork without fighting glare. At 6am you'll likely have most of the railing to yourself; by 10am it's a different place entirely.

Square du Vert-Galant

Surrounded by water on three sides, it catches early light differently than the streets around it, and at dawn you'll typically see a handful of joggers and no one else. It won't hand you the postcard Eiffel Tower shot, but if you want quiet water, old stone, and nobody in the frame, this is the more reliable pick over Trocadéro.

The steps up to Sacré-Cœur

The basilica itself doesn't open until 6:30am, but the parvis and the 270 public steps on rue Foyatier are outdoor space, accessible before that. Climbing them for first light over the rooftops means arriving well ahead of the tour groups that fill the square by mid-morning — one of the only times you'll get the panorama without a wall of people between you and the railing.

Romantic walks in Paris

Île de la Cité to Île Saint-Louis

Start: Square du Vert-Galant. End: the square behind Notre-Dame. About 30–40 minutes at a slow pace, not counting stops.

From Vert-Galant, walk up onto Pont Neuf and cross to Île Saint-Louis via the pedestrian-only Pont Saint-Louis. The island itself is almost entirely quiet residential streets — rue Saint-Louis en l'Île is the only one with much foot traffic — which is a real contrast to how dense the crowds get a few hundred meters away near Notre-Dame. If you time it right, stop at Berthillon for ice cream: it's closed Mondays and Tuesdays, and for roughly five weeks every summer, so it's worth checking rather than building the walk around it and finding it shut. Finish at the square just behind the cathedral, looking at the restored spire.

Canal Saint-Martin

Start: République. End: Bassin de la Villette or wherever you feel like stopping. Flexible length — the canal runs about 4.5km total.

Walk the canal along either Quai de Valmy or Quai de Jemmapes — the two run parallel on opposite banks, and one side is usually sunnier than the other depending on the time of day. The route crosses five cast-iron footbridges and passes nine working locks, lined with plane and chestnut trees, and it's noticeably more residential and less touristy than anything along the Seine. Good for early evening, before dinner at one of the wine bars that cluster around the République end.

A simple couples' itinerary

A loose framework, not a schedule to run on. Leave slack — the best parts of a Paris trip are usually the parts that weren't planned.

Day 1 — First impressions

Morning around the Louvre and Palais Royal (go early or go late — midday is the crowd peak). Afternoon walking the Left Bank quays toward Île Saint-Louis. Evening: dinner, then either a sunset cruise or Trocadéro for the first sparkle of the tower after dark.

Day 2 — Slower and more local

Morning in the Marais or along Canal Saint-Martin — this is the day to sit at a café for longer than feels necessary. Afternoon at Palais Royal's gardens or a museum, whichever fits the pace you're actually keeping by this point. If you haven't been to Trocadéro yet, sunset here is the other good window.

Day 3 — Montmartre, then whatever's left

Go up to Sacré-Cœur early, before the tour groups arrive — it's a different neighborhood at 8am than at 1pm. Keep the rest of the day open for whatever from the earlier days you didn't get to, rather than adding somewhere new.

What to wear for a Paris photoshoot

General styling guidance for anyone planning a couple's photoshoot in Paris, whether it's with a hired photographer or just each other's phones.

Shoes you can actually walk in

Central Paris is cobblestones, worn stone steps, and gravel paths in most gardens — heels sink into the gaps between paving stones and get unstable on wet stone fast. Comfortable, already-broken-in shoes will do more for how relaxed you look in photos than almost anything else on this list.

Layer for weather that changes mid-shoot

Paris weather can shift over the course of a few hours, especially outside summer. A jacket or coat you'd actually want in the photos — not just a windbreaker to shed the second you arrive — covers you either way.

Colors that hold up against a grey-stone backdrop

Most of central Paris is pale limestone and muted rooftops. Warmer or more saturated tones — rust, terracotta, sage, deep navy, burgundy — tend to stand out clearly against that; stark white can blow out in bright sun, and all-black can lose all its shape in shadow. Neither is off-limits, just worth knowing going in.

Coordinate, don't match

Identical colors or patterns on both people tend to read as flat and a little stiff in photos. Picking complementary tones — or just staying in the same general palette while wearing different silhouettes — almost always looks more natural than deliberately matching.

Skip large logos and busy prints

Big logos and small, busy patterns pull the eye away from the two of you and can look dated faster than plain fabrics. Simpler silhouettes tend to age better in photos you'll still like in ten years.
