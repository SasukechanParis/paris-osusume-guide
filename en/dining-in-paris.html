How dining in Paris actually works

French restaurant etiquette isn't complicated, but a few things run differently enough from the US or UK that it's worth knowing before you sit down. None of it requires French — Bonjour (bohn-ZHOOR, "hello"), Merci (mehr-SEE, "thank you"), and S'il vous plaît (seel voo PLEH, "please") will carry you through almost any interaction.

Is tap water free?

Yes, and it's perfectly safe to drink. If a server asks about water and you say nothing, the default assumption is often that you want bottled water (eau plate for still, eau gazeuse for sparkling) — which isn't free. To get free tap water, ask for "une carafe d'eau, s'il vous plaît" (ewn kah-RAHF doh, seel voo PLEH). No one will think it's an odd request; it's normal and common.

Do I need to tip?

No. By French law, a 15% service charge is already built into the price on the menu (service compris) — there's no obligation to add anything on top, and most card machines don't even have a tip-entry step. If service was genuinely great, rounding up the bill or leaving a couple of euros in cash is a nice gesture, not an expectation. This is a real difference from US tipping culture, where 18-20%+ is the norm — in Paris, nobody is quietly judging you for leaving nothing extra.

Can I split the bill?

Usually, yes — it's just not automatic the way it can be in the US. Most restaurants in Paris now bring a portable card reader to the table, and it's genuinely common to pay in turns: each person tells the server how much they're covering and taps their own card. It's not a guaranteed legal right, though — a restaurant is allowed to ask for one payment per table as long as they say so upfront, so for a larger group it's worth mentioning "we'd like to split the bill, is that possible?" when you sit down rather than assuming it at the end.

Do I have to order three courses?

No, not at a typical bistro or brasserie — ordering only a main course, or only a starter and a coffee, is completely normal and the server won't blink. A formule (a fixed combination — usually starter+main or main+dessert, sometimes all three) tends to be cheaper than ordering the same items separately, but it's an option on the menu, not a requirement. The one exception is higher-end tasting-menu restaurants, where the multi-course menu dégustation genuinely is the only thing on offer — that's usually obvious from the restaurant's style and price point before you sit down.

Do I need to book? Can I just sit down?

Casual cafés and brasseries generally take walk-ins without issue. Popular restaurants — especially for dinner on a Friday or Saturday — are a different story, and can be fully booked days or weeks out. When in doubt, reserve; TheFork (the French equivalent of OpenTable, sometimes still called by its old name La Fourchette) is the most widely used booking app and often has discount seatings.

One habit that catches visitors off guard: you generally don't seat yourself. Wait near the entrance until a server acknowledges you, then say how many you are — "Bonjour, une table pour deux, s'il vous plaît" (bohn-ZHOOR, ewn TAH-bluh poor duh, seel voo PLEH — "hello, a table for two please"). Walking straight to an open table and sitting down can come across as presumptuous at some restaurants, even if the room looks half-empty. If you do need to cancel a reservation, a quick call or a cancellation through the booking app is considered good manners — restaurants do track repeated no-shows.

What time do Parisians eat dinner?

Later than you might expect from a US visit, closer to UK habits — dinner service typically runs from around 19:00 to late, and 20:00-21:00 is a normal, not-late, time to sit down. Lunch runs roughly 12:00-14:30. Between lunch and dinner service, many restaurants close entirely for a few hours (the "coupure" or mid-day break) rather than serving food continuously all afternoon the way a US diner might — so a 16:00 craving for a sit-down meal is more reliably solved by a café or bakery than a restaurant.

Why does service feel different from the US or UK?

The most common adjustment for American visitors specifically: the check isn't brought automatically when the meal ends. In France, table time is considered yours for the evening — bringing the bill unprompted can read as rushing the guest, which is close to rude. When you're ready, catch your server's eye and ask: "L'addition, s'il vous plaît" (lah-dee-SYOHN, seel voo PLEH — "the bill, please"). Related to this: servers generally don't check in repeatedly ("how's everything tasting?") the way US servers are trained to — it's not neglect, it's a different, lower-interruption idea of good service. Terrace seating is usually the same price as inside, weather permitting, and dress codes outside upscale restaurants are casual — you don't need to dress up, though flip-flops and beachwear will get you a look.

Menu words worth knowing

entrée — starter (not the main course, unlike US usage) · plat — main course · dessert — dessert · formule — a fixed combination of courses, usually cheaper than ordering separately · menu — in France this means a fixed set meal, not the list of dishes (that's the carte) · café gourmand — a coffee served with a small plate of mini desserts, a nice way to end a meal without committing to a full dessert.
