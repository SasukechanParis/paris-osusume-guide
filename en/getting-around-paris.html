Which metro app, and how tickets work

Use Île-de-France Mobilités, not just the RATP app

RATP (the operator) has its own app, Bonjour RATP, which is fine for checking line-by-line service status. But most travelers find Île-de-France Mobilités (free, iPhone/Android) easier to use day-to-day — it covers every mode in the region (metro, bus, RER, suburban trains) in one place, and handles both ticket purchase and your Navigo Easy card management.

Paper tickets are being phased out

The classic paper metro ticket is being retired line by line through 2026, so plan on one of two options. Option 1: buy a reusable Navigo Easy card (€2) from a station machine, then load it with tickets via the app or machine — a single ride is €2.55, or you can load a discounted 10-ride pack for €17.35. Option 2: if you have an NFC-capable iPhone (XS or later) or Android phone (8.0+), you can buy tickets directly in the Île-de-France Mobilités app and tap your phone at the gate — no physical card needed. Either way, keep your ticket or card until you're out of the station; inspectors do check.

Pickpockets and phone theft — a realistic take

Paris reports more pickpocketing than any other French city, and it's concentrated in predictable places: metro lines 1, 4, and 6, major interchange stations (Châtelet-Les Halles, Gare du Nord), the Montmartre/Sacré-Cœur area, and around the Eiffel Tower. None of this means Paris is unusually dangerous — it's the same low-level opportunistic theft you'd find in any major tourist city — but a few habits help: keep bags in front of you rather than on your back in crowds, and keep valuables in a zipped inner pocket rather than an outer one.

Phones specifically get targeted for grab-and-run theft, including while people are filming or taking photos. A phone case or strap that tethers to your bag or belt loop is a cheap, unglamorous fix that also stops you from dropping the phone mid-photo. Two things worth doing before you land, not after: back up your photos and contacts, and know how to remotely lock/erase your phone (Apple's "Find My" or Google's "Find My Device") and how to suspend your line with your carrier — looking this up while standing in a Paris police station is a bad time to learn it.

Public toilets in Paris

Paris has a genuinely good, underrated system for this: over 400 free, self-cleaning street toilets called sanisettes (the gray or green freestanding booths), positioned near major sights, metro stations, and parks. They're free, open 24 hours in most cases, and clean themselves between uses (the door won't reopen until the cycle finishes, which can take about a minute). If you can't spot one nearby, your fastest fallback is usually a department store, a museum lobby (before the ticket check), a park, or a McDonald's/café — ordering a coffee is the polite move if you use a café's facilities without being a customer.

Luggage storage

Station lockers

Gare du Nord has automatic lockers on the lower level near platform 3 / the rue de Maubeuge exit, open 6:00-23:15 including holidays. Gare de Lyon has lockers in Hall 3 (lower level, near the rue de Bercy exit), open 6:15-22:00 including holidays. Pricing is by locker size — roughly €5.50 small, €7.50 medium, €9.50 large, per 24-hour period.

At Charles de Gaulle airport

CDG has an official left-luggage service run by Bagages du Monde, in Terminal 2 at the TGV/RER station level 4 (across from the Sheraton), open 7:00-21:00 (until 21:30 seasonally).

App-based storage services

Services like Stasher and Bounce (which acquired Nannybag in 2026, though the Nannybag service still operates) partner with cafés and shops around the city to hold bags — often more flexible on hours than a station locker. Book through the app and check current pricing before you go, since rates vary by location.
